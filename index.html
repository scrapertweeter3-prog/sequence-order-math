The same average return is not the same retirement

Two retirees, a million dollars each, withdrawing $40,000 a year. They live through the same three market years, minus 15 percent, minus 5 percent, plus 30 percent, in the opposite order. Geometrically both portfolios average the same 1.63 percent a year. A working calculator says they should end year three in the same place. They are $35,100 apart.

Without withdrawals the order is irrelevant: compounding multiplies, and both retirees would sit at exactly $1,049,750 after year three. Multiplication has no memory of sequence. The $40,000 leaving each account every year is what breaks the symmetry, because a fixed dollar withdraws the same whether the balance is riding a loss or a gain.

Retiree A eats the minus 15 first. Year one ends at $810,000; the loss lands on the full million and the withdrawal still leaves the same day. Retiree B takes the plus 30 first and ends year one at $1,260,000. Same markets, same average, a $450,000 swing in twelve months. By year three A holds $908,350 to B's $943,450, and the gap is permanent: run both forward 27 more years at 6.5 percent with the same $40,000 withdrawal and A finishes near $2.22 million to B's $2.41 million, roughly $192,000 of missing retirement from nothing but ordering.

Sequence risk is not a market property. It is the interaction of a fixed withdrawal with returns, which is why it only exists once you start spending. The withdrawal itself is the mechanism: taken before a down year, that $40,000 never gets to compound through the recovery. A is still short of the starting million seven years in, the better part of a decade of recovery that a different ordering of the identical markets would have skipped.

The guard is boring: once withdrawals start, the first years matter more than the average ever will, and a cash buffer sized to cover the spending through the first down market does more for a plan than another half point of expected return.
